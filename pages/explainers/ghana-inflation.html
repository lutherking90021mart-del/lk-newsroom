How Ghana measures inflation — and what the number means for your household

A plain-language guide to the Consumer Price Index, inflation rates and how to read Ghana’s official price statistics.

LK Newsroom Explainer Desk
Published 8 November 2026 · Explainer
Based on cited public sources

GHANA ECONOMY · PUBLIC DATA GUIDE

When a headline says inflation has fallen, many households quite reasonably ask why food, transport, rent or school costs still feel expensive. The key is to separate the rate at which prices are changing from the level prices have already reached. Ghana’s Consumer Price Index (CPI) is designed to measure changes in the general price level of a representative basket of goods and services that households consume. It is not a list of the exact prices paid by every family.

What the Consumer Price Index measures

The Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) compiles the CPI from prices of selected goods and services. The basket covers different parts of household spending, including food and non-food items and services. Each item contributes according to its expenditure weight. This matters because a price movement in something households spend a large share of their budgets on can have a different effect on the overall index from a movement in an item that accounts for a small share.

An index is a way to summarise many price observations. It is normally compared with a reference or base period. The GSS technical guide explains that the index shows relative price change over time; it does not tell you the actual cash price of an item. A CPI value of 110, for example, should not be read as “everything costs 10 per cent more today”. It is an index value relative to its stated base period and basket.

Inflation is a change in the index, not the index itself

Inflation is calculated from the percentage change in the CPI between two periods. A year-on-year rate compares the index with the same month a year earlier. A month-on-month rate compares it with the immediately preceding month. The two rates answer different questions, so a news report should always make clear which one it is discussing.

Imagine, using hypothetical numbers, that the price index rises from 100 to 120 over one period and then rises from 120 to 126 over the next comparable period. The index is still increasing, but the pace of increase is slower in the second period. That is disinflation: prices are still rising on average, just more slowly. A sustained fall in the overall price index is a different situation, commonly described as deflation.

Why the published rate may not match one household’s experience

No two households buy exactly the same basket. A family that spends a large share of its income on food may feel food-price changes more strongly than a household whose spending is weighted towards rent, transport or services. Location also matters: prices and the mix of goods purchased can differ between regions and between urban and rural areas. The national CPI is a statistical average designed to represent household consumption; it is not a personal inflation calculator for every reader.

This is why it is useful to look beyond one headline number. The GSS publishes CPI information by product group and region. Food and non-food movements can point in different directions. A national average can also hide variation between regions. When explaining a monthly release, the most helpful questions are: which groups moved most, which regions differed, and how does the latest change compare with the previous month and the same month last year?

How readers can check the figures

  1. Find the release month and confirm whether the headline is year-on-year or month-on-month.
  2. Look at the overall CPI and then open the food, non-food and other available product-group series.
  3. Check the region selection where the statistical table provides it. Do not assume that the national average describes every town.
  4. Read the GSS notes on the reference period and methodology before comparing long historical series. When the statistical agency rebases an index, comparisons should use the agency’s consistent series.
  5. Compare the official release with the prices that matter to your own household budget. The official rate is a national measure; your experience can differ.

Why the distinction matters

Confusing lower inflation with falling prices can lead to poor public discussion. A slower increase can be positive news for price stability, but it does not automatically restore the purchasing power households lost when prices rose earlier. Wages, incomes, borrowing costs, exchange rates, supply conditions and the prices of individual essentials all influence how people experience the economy. The CPI helps describe one important part of that picture; it does not explain every cause or every household outcome by itself.

For responsible reporting, a headline about inflation should identify the period being compared, state whether it is the annual or monthly measure, and point readers to the detailed breakdown. Readers should be able to open the original GSS release and inspect the data rather than rely on a single figure without context.
